# Timestamped notes: four ways to live off $1 million

**Source:** [I Tested 4 Ways to Live Off $1 Million in Retirement — Here’s What Won](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IHm81JJyZX8)  
**Creator:** Antonio Cibella, Vision Wealth Management  
**Published:** September 11, 2026  
**Length:** 30:33

These are detailed paraphrased notes made from the video's automatic captions. They preserve the substance and sequence without reproducing the full copyrighted transcript. Figures and results below are the presenter's reported hypothetical backtest results; they have not been independently reproduced.

## 00:00–03:59 — The household and the test

- The presenter asks why identical $1 million portfolios can produce very different retirement experiences. The answer is the withdrawal and spending system, not just the starting balance.
- The hypothetical couple retires at 65 and receives about $5,400 per month from Social Security. They want roughly $9,000 per month after tax.
- Taxes are deliberately omitted from the demonstration. The portfolio must initially provide about $47,000 per year.
- Four strategies are compared over 69 overlapping rolling 30-year historical periods through 2025, with an additional 35-year longevity test and close attention to unfavorable starting years.
- The four approaches are:
  1. a conventional 60/40 portfolio with inflation-adjusted withdrawals;
  2. an income-first portfolio;
  3. guaranteed lifetime income combined with a smaller portfolio; and
  4. time-segmented buckets combined with dynamic spending guardrails.
- The presenter's “paycheck score” considers depletion, income reliability, performance in bad starting periods, total lifetime spending, ending wealth, and flexibility.

## 04:00–06:59 — Strategy 1: conventional 60/40 withdrawals

- The portfolio begins at 60% stocks and 40% bonds, is rebalanced annually, and pays $47,000 in year one. Later withdrawals rise with inflation.
- The central danger is sequence-of-returns risk: poor returns early in retirement combine with withdrawals to remove capital before it can recover.
- The presenter reports survival in roughly four of every five 30-year periods, but only three of ten especially difficult starting periods.
- Reported paycheck score: **63**.
- Interpretation: the approach is simple and can work well in favorable sequences, but it has no built-in mechanism to reduce withdrawals before damage becomes irreversible.

## 07:00–11:59 — Strategy 2: income first

- This portfolio is described as approximately 65% dividend-oriented stocks and 35% bonds. Spending is limited to dividends and interest, avoiding forced principal sales.
- The presenter reports that it survives every tested 30- and 35-year period and produces a median inflation-adjusted ending value of about $1.8 million.
- The trade-off is unreliable lifestyle funding. The natural income meets the desired amount only a little more than half the time, implying a pay cut in almost half of retirement years.
- Reported paycheck score: **85**.
- Interpretation: it scores well for capital preservation and legacy goals but may force the household to live below its desired budget. “Never sell principal” is not the same as “reliably fund the target lifestyle.”

## 12:00–15:59 — Strategy 3: lifetime income plus portfolio

- In the example, $250,000 purchases joint lifetime income paying 6.2%, or $15,500 per year. The remaining $750,000 stays in a 60/40 portfolio.
- The lifetime-income payment is level rather than inflation-adjusted, so its purchasing power declines. The smaller liquid portfolio must fund the remaining gap and absorb inflation.
- The presenter reports survival in about 75% of periods and only two of ten difficult starting periods.
- Reported paycheck score: **59**.
- Interpretation: the income product performs the narrow job promised—continuing its payment—but does not eliminate sequence risk or inflation risk from the household's whole plan. Liquid assets can still be exhausted.

## 16:00–18:59 — Strategy 4: buckets plus guardrails

- The $1 million is segmented by time horizon:
  - **$94,000 cash**, about two years of the initial $47,000 portfolio need;
  - **$235,000 stable income**, about five more years of the initial need; and
  - **$671,000 long-term growth**.
- Regular withdrawals come from cash. The stable bucket bridges extended weak markets, while the growth bucket is intended to replenish shorter-term reserves when conditions are favorable.
- The purpose is behavioral and operational: reduce the need to sell growth assets immediately after a decline. The video does not define the securities in the stable bucket or a fully mechanical refill trigger.
- The simplified spending guardrails begin with a 4.7% withdrawal rate:
  - if the proposed withdrawal divided by the current portfolio is **20% above** the starting rate, spending is cut **10%**;
  - if it is **20% below** the starting rate, spending is raised **10%**.
- For a 4.7% starting rate, those trigger rates are **5.64%** and **3.76%**.
- The presenter says the client process is more nuanced; the video intentionally uses simpler rules to make the historical comparison transparent.

## 19:00–20:59 — Reported aggregate result

- The presenter reports that buckets plus guardrails survive all tested 30- and 35-year periods.
- The median inflation-adjusted ending balance is about $1.4 million.
- Average lifetime inflation-adjusted spending is reported to be about $378,000 higher than the fixed-withdrawal baseline because the rules permit increases in favorable conditions.
- A 1951 starting example reportedly creates about $89,000 of additional spending and still finishes with around $1 million.
- Reported paycheck score: **87**, the highest of the four.

## 21:00–25:59 — Stress case: retirement beginning in 1966

- This start date exposes the portfolios to an especially difficult combination of weak markets and inflation.
- The conventional 60/40 portfolio reportedly runs out around year 20.
- The lifetime-income combination reportedly exhausts liquid portfolio assets around year 19, although the contracted lifetime payment continues.
- The income-first approach survives with roughly $750,000 in real assets but supplies only about 65% of the target income.
- Buckets plus guardrails reportedly finish with around $740,000 in real assets after **eight spending cuts and three increases**.
- The presenter's lesson is that flexibility creates an early warning and response system. The result is not painless: survival is purchased partly through lower spending during stress.

## 26:00–27:59 — Favorable case: retirement beginning in 1988

- A fixed 60/40 withdrawal plan reportedly finishes above $3 million in real terms, suggesting the retiree could have spent more.
- The income-first portfolio reportedly finishes around $3.7 million.
- Buckets plus guardrails generate **seven increases and no cuts**, about $677,000 in additional spending, and still finish above the starting real value.
- The lesson is symmetrical: guardrails are intended to prevent both unsustainably high withdrawals in bad periods and unnecessarily low spending in good periods.

## 28:00–30:33 — Ranking and conclusion

- Final reported scores:
  - buckets plus guardrails: **87**;
  - income first: **85**;
  - conventional 60/40: **63**;
  - lifetime income plus portfolio: **59**.
- The winner is not simply the strategy with the highest ending balance. It is the strategy that best balances near-term liquidity, long-horizon growth, dependable spending, adaptability, and legacy value under the presenter's scoring system.
- The closing message is that $1 million is an input, not a retirement plan. A plan also needs rules governing where withdrawals come from and how spending responds to changing conditions.

## Important limitations

- Historical rolling periods overlap and therefore are not 69 independent observations.
- The video omits taxes and does not fully specify fees, product expenses, trading costs, account types, RMDs, Medicare premium effects, long-term care, or the exact bucket holdings and refill rules.
- A backtest benefits from known historical data and does not prove future success.
- The 4.7% rate and two-plus-five-year bucket sizes are example assumptions, not universal recommendations.
- The video's simplified guardrails do not fully define how temporary cuts are restored, whether thresholds reset, or how repeated actions compound.
